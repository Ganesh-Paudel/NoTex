\begin{conceptbox}
Average speed describes the total distance traveled per unit of time.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{notationbox}
Let d represent distance, t represent elapsed time, and v represent average speed.
\end{notationbox}

\begin{objectivebox}
By the end of this lesson, calculate average speed and explain the units in your answer.
\end{objectivebox}

\begin{contextbox}
Measuring motion helps us compare journeys, plan travel times, and describe experiments.
\end{contextbox}

\begin{connectionbox}
Average speed connects the slope of a distance-time graph to a physical measurement.
\end{connectionbox}

\begin{summarybox}
To calculate average speed, divide total distance by total elapsed time and include units.
\end{summarybox}

\begin{question}
A cyclist travels 120 meters in 10 seconds. What is the cyclist's average speed?
\end{question}

\begin{answer}
The cyclist's average speed is 12 meters per second.
\end{answer}

\begin{examplebox}
A walker covers 30 meters in 15 seconds, so the average speed is 2 meters per second.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{solutionbox}
Step 1: Identify the distance as 120 meters and the time as 10 seconds.
Step 2: Divide 120 by 10 to obtain 12.
Step 3: Report the average speed as 12 meters per second.
\end{solutionbox}

\begin{practicebox}
A runner covers 200 meters in 25 seconds. Calculate the average speed and state its units.
\end{practicebox}

\begin{recallbox}
Without looking at your notes, explain the difference between distance and displacement.
\end{recallbox}

\begin{hintbox}
Start with average speed = total distance / total time.
\end{hintbox}

\begin{misconceptionbox}
Average speed is not always the arithmetic mean of two speeds; the time spent at each speed matters.
\end{misconceptionbox}

\begin{askbox}
How does average speed differ from instantaneous speed on a journey with several stops?
\end{askbox}

\begin{actionbox}
Complete three speed problems and check that each final answer includes units.
\end{actionbox}

\begin{reflectionbox}
I can calculate average speed, but I need more practice interpreting changes in graph slope.
\end{reflectionbox}

\begin{formulabox}
Average speed = total distance / total elapsed time.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{theorembox}
For constant speed, distance is proportional to elapsed time: doubling the time doubles the distance.
\end{theorembox}

\begin{proofbox}
At constant speed v, the distance after time t is v * t. After time 2 * t, the distance is v * 2 * t, which is twice the original distance.
\end{proofbox}

\begin{derivationbox}
Start with v = d / t. Multiplying both sides by t gives d = v * t.
\end{derivationbox}

\begin{assumptionbox}
Assume the cyclist moves at constant speed and the measured elapsed time is greater than zero.
\end{assumptionbox}

\begin{givenbox}
Given: distance = 120 meters and time = 10 seconds. Find: average speed in meters per second.
\end{givenbox}

\begin{unitsbox}
Distance is measured in meters (m), time in seconds (s), and speed in meters per second (m/s).
\end{unitsbox}

\begin{checkbox}
Check the result: 12 meters per second multiplied by 10 seconds gives the original 120 meters.
\end{checkbox}

\begin{specialcasebox}
If elapsed time is zero, distance divided by time is undefined and cannot be used to calculate speed.
\end{specialcasebox}

\begin{hypothesisbox}
A toy car released from a higher ramp position will have a greater average speed over the measured track.
\end{hypothesisbox}

\begin{variablesbox}
Independent variable: release height. Dependent variable: average speed. Controls: car, track length, and track surface.
\end{variablesbox}

\begin{equipmentbox}
Use a toy car, an adjustable ramp, a meter ruler, a stopwatch, and a notebook.
\end{equipmentbox}

\begin{procedurebox}
1. Measure a 1-meter section of track.
2. Release the car without pushing it.
3. Record the travel time over the measured section.
4. Repeat three times at each release height.
\end{procedurebox}

\begin{observationbox}
The car reached the end of the measured section sooner when released from the higher position.
\end{observationbox}

\begin{databox}
Example travel times over 1 meter: trial 1 = 1.2 seconds; trial 2 = 1.1 seconds; trial 3 = 1.3 seconds.
\end{databox}

\begin{uncertaintybox}
Manual stopwatch timing introduces reaction-time uncertainty; repeated trials help reveal variation.
\end{uncertaintybox}

\begin{analysisbox}
The mean travel time is 1.2 seconds. Dividing 1 meter by 1.2 seconds gives an estimated average speed of 0.83 meters per second.
\end{analysisbox}

\begin{conclusionbox}
The example results support the hypothesis that a higher release position increases the car's average speed.
\end{conclusionbox}
